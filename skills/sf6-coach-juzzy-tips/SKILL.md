# Juzz the Tip (Coach Juzzy)

> **Installed skills only.** This plugin ships the analysis, prediction, dashboard, glossary, character notes, Drive efficiency, Juzzy and Maj skills, and .shog's coaching rules as an example. Other coaching skills named in this text (E.X. Dojo, Coosco, DoctaAfrikan) aren't included: load them only if the player has installed them, otherwise skip those checks.


Source: Juzzy (YouTube @juzz87), a 1600 MR Ken who runs the "Sit Down and Shut Up" (SDASU) coaching sessions on the FightRise Discord. "Juzz the Tip" videos are short clips cut from those sessions, 19 videos from January 2025 to September 2026. Each section below is my own paraphrase of one video, with its link. The sf6-replay-analysis skill loads this for every review; sf6-coach-juzzy-sessions covers the recurring themes of the full sessions and set reviews. Shared with Juzzy's permission.

Juzzy coaches at Platinum to around 1700 MR, so his advice is aimed at players who still lose to neutral skips, overextension and autopilot. He calls himself a feel-based player and often says "this is what works for me". Treat it as a coach's opinion, not frame-data law, and check his examples against the frame data for the characters in the replay.

## Neutral

### Neutral skips first, footsies second
Video: "Neutral Game and Footsies in SF6?" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akcQZDydIjM)
- Footsies only exist once both players have stopped skipping neutral: jumps, raw Drive Rush, Dragonlash, Bison scissors, Honda headbutt, Blanka ball. Until the opponent respects you, the job is to check those skips.
- After that, footsies are about getting a whiff punish that leads to a knockdown.
- In the Akuma v Ken set review he repeats it: below Master most players can't play a grounded neutral yet, so simply anti-airing, checking Drive Rush and the other skips, and blocking on wake-up wins most games.

### Space decides the button
Video: "How Space Impacts the Neutral Game" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S-J_QX-GtpI)
- In neutral, space chooses the button. Frame data matters less there than up close.
- The closer the opponent gets, the faster the button you use. At range, use cover attacks with disjointed hitboxes against buttons that stick out a hurtbox.
- Walking in and pressing only works once the opponent is afraid to press.
- A safe resting spot is just outside most characters' longest normals, about round-start distance.

### Using space to force a game plan
Video: "Using Space to Force a Game Plan" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LiDKGHEKB-Y)
- Juzzy held a Kimberly at a range outside both players' normals, because the student was uncomfortable winning ground interactions. That left her only slides, teleports and jumps, which he could react to.
- If the opponent keeps giving you space without contesting it, walk forward and take it. You gain screen position for free, and they have to answer once they reach the corner.
- Watch how the opponent responds when you walk forward, walk back or jump. Their answer tells you what range they want to play at.
- Higher players adapt within a game or two, so be ready for them to change the range on you. Footsies are "more art than science": every counter works only until they change what they do.

### Building a neutral plan (Cammy example)
Video: "Trying to Build a Neutral Game Plan" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQqsbw5GM7I)
- Control space with 5HK at max range (−3, hard to punish there) and 5MK.
- He frames footsies as three choices: walk in and press, a cover attack, or bait and whiff punish.
- These are lower risk than Hooligan and Spin Knuckle. Establish the ground game first, like the run game before play action; the volatile tools work better once the opponent respects the buttons.
- Drive Impact punishes uncancelable buttons when you overuse them.

### The don't-whiff drill
Video: "Don't Whiff Drill" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qk7sYv71JBs)
SF6 buttons have more recovery than in older games, so whiffing costs more. Pick the 2-3 buttons you actually use in neutral, then:
1. **Static:** stand at a distance, predict whether the button reaches, press, and repeat until you can tell the tip range by eye.
2. **Walking:** walk in and out and press the moment it will connect. The gap between the characters closes at a fixed rate, so you learn when it will and won't land.
3. **Moving dummy:** record random dummy movement and do the same. Real opponents are less random, and walk speeds differ by character (Akuma vs Zangief vs Cammy).
4. **Real games:** play casuals with one goal, never whiff. Hit or block doesn't matter. Expect to lose a lot at first.
Once you stop whiffing, the opponent has to do something else (bait you, jump, Drive Rush) to open you up.

### Don't land minus buttons too close
Video: "Landing minus buttons too close" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0jdKRzXZvs)
- Avoid whiffing heavies, but also avoid landing them point-blank. Ken's 5HP blocked point-blank let Juzzy jab: it beats a slower follow-up or a non-string special (it interrupted a heavy Jinrai), loses nothing to Drive Rush (the freeze eats the jab) and does nothing against a true blockstring.
- Further away, the same jab whiffs and pressing there makes no sense. Spacing your blocked heavy at the tip takes that option away.

## Offense

### Pressing buttons with intention
Video: "Pressing Buttons With Intention" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zh2bSmk1PXo)
- Decide what you'll do with a button before you press it, e.g. 5HP into Drive Rush on hit. That frees your mental stack to watch the opponent.
- Have a small flowchart for each button: what happens on hit, on block and on whiff, with a safe exit.
- Pressing fewer buttons, each with a purpose, beats pressing many.

### Enforcing plus frames
Video: "Enforcing Plus Frames" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UvUbPGGUkI)
- At +3 (e.g. Drive Rush jab), strike or throw beats mashing. Enforce that first, so the opponent learns they can't press.
- Once they respect it, you can leave gaps, use bigger buttons and walk-up throws.
- In the Cammy v JP set review: "when you have plus frames, enforce them at least at first", discourage mashing, then go for the other stuff.

### Overextending
Video: "Overextending" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1HTxCXNTsPY)
- With the corner, a life lead and meter, consider backing off instead of pouring on meaties and Drive Impacts. Endless pressure eventually meets a reversal or a block-out, as in the Cammy v Guile example where a Level 1 caught a repeated Drive Impact.
- A cornered player with less life has to act: press a badly spaced button, jump into your DP, or Drive Rush into a check. Backing off makes those easy to answer, since you're watching for one or two things instead of reacting raw.
- Reduce volatility. Platinum is full of high-risk, high-reward players who turn rounds around with one confirm.

### Autopilot: pros and cons
Video: "Autopiloting Pros and Cons" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kzEil_CJ_bo)
- Autopilot lowers your mental stack but loses to whatever the autopilot doesn't cover. Example: a Rashid at +1 pressing a 6f 2MK into a Bison mashing a 4f jab; +1 doesn't help when their button is 2f faster than yours.
- Shimmy then react, instead of autopiloting a big punish: back off and watch. A throw tech gets 5HK, a parry gets a walk-up throw, a jump gets a DP. An autopiloted 5HK loses to a forward jump cross-up.
- If you want to autopilot anyway, use a faster, smaller button (5MP instead of 5HK) that still converts and recovers in time to anti-air or block.

### Breaking your own autopilot
Video: "Breaking your own autopilot" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-JmKWsAH_Sk)
- After dropping about 300 MR on autopiloted 2MK~Drive Rush, Juzzy spent a ranked session winning with one button (Ken 5HP) and never whiffing it. It forced him to watch spacing and the opponent's habits.
- Combos and oki should stay on autopilot; neutral decisions shouldn't. Limiting yourself on purpose (casuals, custom rooms) brings the conscious decision back.

### Taking turns
Video: "Taking Turns" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C8G3iLkvVes)
- SF6 isn't a simple "I'm minus, so it's your turn" game. Most buttons are minus, but pushback decides whether the defender can reach.
- Chained lights shrink the gap, so mashing between them gets frame-trapped.
- A max-range Ken 5HK at −5 is hard to punish: 5f buttons may not reach, and a slower one can be whiff-punished by 5HP.
- After a big minus button, the defender must pick a button that reaches in time. Too big (a 12-14f heavy) gets clipped; too small whiffs.

## Defense

### The goal of defending okizeme
Video: "The Goal of Defending Okizeme" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8GwZjsUGpG8)
- The goal is to stop the pressure and get back to neutral, not to avoid all damage.
- Oki is a guessing game whose risk/reward favours the attacker, so pick the option that gets you out most reliably.

### Decision making in mixups
Video: "Decision Making In Mixup Situations" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wPibpsyLITM)
- Defend against what the opponent has shown, not against options they haven't used. Going several layers deep beats the opponent you imagine, not the one in front of you, who usually does the simplest thing.
- Higher-level players take less risk: on knockdown they mostly apply safe pressure and enforce plus frames.
- Decide what you're willing to lose to. "I'll take the throw" and then beat everything else simplifies the mental stack and lets you watch how they structure their offense.
- People aren't random: something that works nine times out of ten will be done again.
- Risk/reward on defense is poor. A correct offensive guess can win the round; a correct defensive guess only gets you out. So take less risk on defense, and avoid the analysis paralysis that leads to just holding block and getting thrown.
- His offense is the mirror image: do what they don't expect next (e.g. shimmy again after they got thrown).

### Covering several options with delay timing
Video: "Covering Multiple Options With Delay Timing" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F44SW_EMD90)
- After a parry, a delayed jab beats a shimmy, a walk-up throw and continued lights.
- The attacker beats it with a deliberate gap into a slower button such as 2MP, so offense has to be layered against option selects.
- After a perfect-parried safe jump the attacker is −2: use a delay tech.
- Keep it simple: at ±2, it's strike or throw.

### Defending against Jinrai (Ken)
Videos: "Defending Against Jinrai" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uAYHboYCCRc) and its remix (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62vJvVco0hU)
- A delayed 2LP, timed to the low follow-up, beats the nothing and the overhead follow-ups and blocks the low (which is −5 and punishable).
- Mashing Drive Impact beats 2MK into Jinrai, but it doesn't come out against the airtight light Jinrai, which is −11 and punishable anyway.

## Resources

### Meter management
Video: "Meter Management" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZjyuYsZ3R4M)
- Check your Drive before extending a punish-counter combo. Take the meterless ~2,900 rather than burning out for +300.
- Cash out with SA3 when it kills: you can't play for round three if you die in round two.
- Under-spending is a leak too.
- Glance at the meter every interaction, like checking the minimap in StarCraft.

### Just use super
Video: "Just Use Super" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gpb6RBN4E4s)
- If a neutral hit converts into a meterless Level 3, spend it mid-round. Super doesn't carry into the next game, and the damage takes away the opponent's Drive and options (the Drive Impact that later won the round might not have happened).
- Spending gains the life lead and Drive back, and puts the opponent behind on Drive. If they have no super, spending yours costs you little.
- Lab a consistent route into Level 3 (or Level 2 when unsure you'll have three bars) from your common hits.

## How to apply this in a replay review
1. **Neutral skips:** count each player's jumps, raw Drive Rushes and other skips, and whether they were checked. Below Master, if {{PLAYER_NAME}} loses to unchecked skips, that comes before footsies advice.
2. **Whiffs:** list {{PLAYER_NAME}}'s whiffed neutral buttons and their range relative to the tip. Several whiffs means recommending the don't-whiff drill with his 2-3 main pokes.
3. **Spacing of blocked minus buttons:** flag heavies blocked point-blank that let the opponent jab or take their turn, and note the range that would have been safe.
4. **Intention and autopilot:** look for the same button sequence repeated regardless of the result (e.g. 2MK~Drive Rush fished every interaction, or a big shimmy punish pressed into a jump). Suggest a smaller button or the one-button drill.
5. **Plus frames:** at {{PLAYER_NAME}}'s plus situations, did he enforce them (strike or throw) before leaving gaps? Did the opponent mash because he never enforced them?
6. **Overextending:** in the corner with a life and meter lead, did {{PLAYER_NAME}} keep pressing into a reversal? Note whether backing off and waiting for them to act was the better plan.
7. **Defensive decisions:** judge each guess against what the opponent had shown so far, and whether {{PLAYER_NAME}} was defending against options they never used. Pair with sf6-the-defense-equation-exdojo.
8. **Meter:** flag burnouts for small extra damage, unspent super at round ends, and hits where a Level 3 cash-out would have killed or swung the round. Pair with sf6-drive-efficiency.
9. **Turns:** when {{PLAYER_NAME}} got hit trying to take his turn back, check the pushback and his button's startup and range; recommend a button that reaches in time or blocking.
