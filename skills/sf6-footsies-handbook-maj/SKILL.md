**It was written for older Street Fighter games** (SF2, Alpha, 3rd Strike, CvS2, SF4). The concepts carry over; many examples don't apply as written to SF6. Each element below has an **SF6** line translating it. When the player or a replay shows that an SF6 translation is wrong or needs a character-specific version, update that line rather than the original idea.

Footsies here means the mid-range ground game: controlling space with walking, pokes and whiff punishes to bait mistakes and punish them. Maj presents it as a set of common situations, each with a basic answer ("elements"), to practise one at a time until they fit naturally into a game plan.

## The five rules
- **Rule #1: Never get predictable.** Everything has a counter, so footsies is constant analysis of the opponent, not a fixed routine. If you lose track of what the opponent is doing, don't fall back on your default plan: play safe, or show something they haven't seen yet.
- **Rule #2: Decide where the fight happens.** Control the distance and the screen position. Example: a Dhalsim who refused to fight at his usual mid range against Bison, waited at long range for one mistake, then didn't let Bison reset.
- **Rule #3: Watch the opponent's state of mind.** Some tactics exploit hesitation, others redirect aggression. Learn what makes each player hesitate or get aggressive. Examples:
  - a nearly broken guard (SF6: low Drive or near burnout) makes people jumpy with reversals and supers;
  - having no meter against a full-meter opponent makes people over-safe;
  - lots of fireballs make some opponents complacent and some angry.
- **Rule #4: Tactics over gimmicks.** The ideal tactic is a 50/50 where both options do similar damage, are similarly safe, leave you in a similar position, and no single defensive option beats both. A gimmick beats the opponent's most common reaction but nothing else, so it works until they see you have nothing behind it. Gimmicks are fine as a supplement; depending on them isn't.
- **Rule #5 (Epilogue): Go all out or don't bother.** Commit fully to footsies or don't play them at all; half-hearted footsies just to survive only delays the loss, because strong players sense the hesitation. Focus on the effort, not on winning or losing. "All out" doesn't mean gambling: a blind super or reversal needs a pattern-based reason.

The comments add **Buktooth's triangle**: sticking out a normal beats walking forward, walking forward beats a whiff-punish attempt, and whiff punishing beats sticking out a normal. Walking is uniquely strong because it has no recovery. This is the same triangle as the footsies triangle in sf6-footsies-101-exdojo and the chess-or-poker rule in sf6-replay-analysis.

## Chapter 1: footsies 101
- **01 Step into poke range and back out.** Step briefly into their poke range and retreat at once to draw a poke, then punish the whiff. Best in tense, cautious stretches and against charge characters who rarely walk forward. **SF6:** the core of neutral. Against charge characters (Guile, Blanka, Dee Jay, E. Honda, M. Bison), a step in and out also tests whether they'll give up charge. Cammy's long whiff punishes (5MK, 2MK~Drive Rush, 5HP) are the payoff.
- **02 Sit just outside their reversal range.** Make your offense or position look punishable while staying just out of their reversal's reach, so they waste meter or a reversal. **SF6:** play at the edge of OD DP, Level 3 and Drive Impact range. A whiffed DI or super leaves them burnt out or without meter. Note their Drive and SA stock first (Rule #3).
- **03 Walk up and throw once they hesitate.** After poking enough that they hesitate to press at a range, walk in and throw. It doesn't work at round start, before you've earned that hesitation. **SF6:** same idea. The throw is 5f with short range (about 0.8 in the capture's distance units), and throw loops in SF6 make it worth more. Pairs with sf6-improving-shimmy-exdojo (throw until they answer it).

## Chapter 2: light feints and reading meter
- **04 Whiff a 2LK just outside their sweep range.** The opponent may mistake it for a bigger poke and counter-poke into nothing; punish the whiff or walk up and throw. Best in mirrors and against players who react well to your medium pokes. **SF6:** whiff a light at the edge of their 2MK or sweep range to draw 2MK~Drive Rush or a sweep, then whiff-punish. Fast-recovering lights (Cammy 2LK, 5LP) are the feint.
- **05 Punish an overused feint.** If they keep feinting, step in and hit with your best combo on their rhythm. **SF6:** a repeated whiffed light at the same range is a whiff-punish window for a medium or 2MK~Drive Rush, especially when they whiff it in the same spot every time.
- **06 Watch behaviour change when they have meter.** Mid-range back-and-forth movement with a full bar often means they're fishing for a super. Test with fast normals, not slow specials, or put them in blockstun and bait the super from a range that looks safe but isn't. **SF6:** watch for players fishing with SA3 or CA (most SA3s are invincible) and for buffered Level 1/2 off pokes. Test with lights and blocked strings, not committal specials.

## Chapter 3: the corner
A cornered player can't retreat, so the attacker decides when the fight is in or out of range. Two approaches: rush them down (your pokes can't whiff), or hold your ground and answer every escape (a war of attrition the attacker wins). The longer someone stays cornered, the more often they lose, and high-level corner exchanges can be 5-10 seconds of nothing because neither player will open a gap.
- **07 Poke the cornered player's toes, then punish the counter-poke.** Stay just outside their reach, poke when they look ready to move or relax, and punish the counter-poke when it whiffs. Veterans step forward before counter-poking, so leave a little extra room.
- **08 Shimmy after a tick throw.** In the corner they can't walk out of throw range, so they must act, which makes them easy to bait.
- **09 Max-range light after a string.** In the corner a max-range light can't whiff, so you lose nothing. It beats their button, and if they block it you can walk up and throw. If they jump, anti-air on reaction.
- **SF6:** the corner matters even more in SF6. A blocked DI in the corner is a wall splat, and a burnt-out player in the corner is close to checkmate. Their escapes are jump, Drive Reversal (2 bars), OD reversal, DI, and back jump against a throw (sf6-the-defense-equation-exdojo). For Cammy, holding the corner with 5LP/2LP checks and throws, and punishing jump-outs with 5HK or Cannon Spike, is the attrition approach.

## Chapter 4: baiting supers
With a life lead and a meter-holding opponent, have eight to ten answers ready per matchup. Ignoring the super threat invites comebacks.
- **10 Bait the fishing opponent.** Stay out for a while, then walk into their 2MK range and immediately block low. If the super comes, punish it; if not, you've neutralised the threat. Keep baits simple: an elaborate bait the opponent doesn't read can eat a Level 3.
- **11 Exploit reversal-super attempts in your strings.** If they try to super at the gaps or link points of your long strings, put a deliberate gap in your string where your counter to their super fits.
- **12 Test their reset before committing.** When an aggressive opponent pushes you out to reset neutral, don't rush back in with a big move. Test with one low jab, or just block, and see what they do.
- **SF6:** "meter" is both Drive (Drive Impact, OD reversals, Drive Reversal) and the SA gauge (SA3/CA). For 10, block and punish a whiffed or blocked SA3, and walk in then block against DI fishing (or answer a seen DI with your own DI or a jab with armor-break timing). For 11, leave a gap where their OD DP or super would come out and block, or use a DP option select (sf6-drive-rush-blockstrings-exdojo). For 12, after a Drive Reversal or pushback reset, check with a jab before committing to Drive Rush.

## Chapter 5: when to jump
"Don't jump" is the default: a jump gives up control for about two seconds and stakes the round's momentum on one guess. Before jumping, ask what you intend to land on. If the answer is "my combo", you're jumping into an uppercut. Jump only when you know what the opponent will do and a jump is the best answer to it.
- **13 Jump over a fireball you've made them want to throw.** Stay grounded long enough (Daigo waited most of two rounds) that they believe you won't jump, then jump the fireball you invited.
- **14 Bait a sweep up close, then cross up.** This works from inside their sweep range, even in the corner. It's a high-commitment read, so learn their sweep habit first.
- **15 Know your jump-in ranges and mix up around them.** Example: a long-reaching jump-in done from max range with no button makes their DP whiff. Air-to-ground footsies (dive kicks, air fireballs) shouldn't be overused; the grounded player usually has better options. Some jumps are fine against characters who force you to the air (zoners).
- **SF6:** jumping is also how you lose to Drive Impact and anti-airs. Use sf6-how-to-jump-exdojo and sf6-air-footsies-exdojo for SF6 specifics. Cammy's j.HP/j.MK and dive kick spacing are the practical versions of Element 15. Jumping a fireball works best against slow, committal projectiles from far away.

## Chapter 6: close range
- **16 Walk in for your light combo.** After a blocked light, a blocked jump-in or a knockdown, walk forward into your 2LK combo range. A walk-in looks like a throw, so many opponents react defensively. If they mash in blockstun, time the light to beat their jab; if they're patient, walk longer to bait a reaction.
- **17 Walk back, then hit the defensive throw.** After a blocked crossup, step back and time a low to hit as they try to tech or counter-throw. It works without a throw-whiff animation. You usually can't confirm it, so end in something safe on block.
- **18 Uppercut them up close.** Once they're afraid to press at close range, walk in freely.
- **SF6:** 16 is a walk-up strike/throw after a plus situation (Drive Rush jab, knockdown). 17 is a delayed strike or shimmy that catches delayed tech (sf6-improving-shimmy-exdojo, sf6-delayed-throw-offense-exdojo). Throw protection on wake-up and after blockstun means timing matters more in SF6.

## Chapter 7: small tricks
- **19 Whiff a 2LK, then a standing light.** The crouching light extends your hurtbox. Chaining into a standing jab (or a high-kicking standing LK) pulls the low hurtbox away, so a low counter-poke passes under it. **SF6:** check the Cancel column ("Chn") for which lights chain on whiff. Use it only if the chain shrinks the hurtbox on the frame data or training-mode hitbox display.
- **20 Earn a free step, then throw.** Use the threat of a strong poke (or a slow fireball travelling ahead of you) to take a step forward, then go for the throw. Counter: walk in immediately and throw first.
- **21 Walk back after blocking a long-range jump-in.** It can make a delayed sweep or follow-up whiff. Use it sparingly; it's not a main plan, and back-dashing there is usually worse.
- **22 Bait the counter-poke at the exact range, standing still.** Poke from the range where their counter-poke barely whiffs. Crouching (and in some games blocking) enlarges your hurtbox, so stand and don't block to make it whiff. **SF6:** check crouch vs stand hurtboxes in training mode before relying on this.
- These are among hundreds of small edges; each one adds up.

## Chapter 8: hop kicks and dive kicks
- **23 Hop kicks as offense.** Hop kicks clear lows, are too fast to anti-air on reaction and move forward, so they're hard to whiff. Their threat pushes opponents into risky anti-air attempts you can punish. Even blocked, they build momentum and meter and push toward the corner.
- **24 Hop kicks up close.** Use them to extend pressure, beat telegraphed poke strings, and (if airborne) beat throws. Their threat makes opponents block passively, which opens throws.
- **25 Dive kicks with no minimum height work like hop kicks.** They need to float over lows and recover faster than an anti-air reaction. SF4 Cammy's Cannon Strike is one of Maj's examples.
- **Principle:** don't try to win with hop kicks or dive kicks alone. Use them just enough to make the opponent's best counters awkward, which opens the rest of your offense.
- **26 Countering them.** Keep them out of their ideal range. Once they're in it, block, read their next move and punish or reposition. A standing jab beats many hop kicks and dive kicks.
- **SF6:** SF6 Cammy's Cannon Strike (j.214K, forward jump only) is the live version of Element 25. It's -8 at worst on block and up to +5 when done low and spaced. Use it as a whiff punish on their poke (Coach-DoctaAfrikan's "scam artist") and at heights that land plus, not as a raw approach. Opponents answer it with standing jabs, 5LP/5LK, DP or a step back and punish (sf6-dps-and-divekicks-exdojo).

## Chapter 9: crossups
Crossups depend on anticipation, not reaction, because ground answers are faster than a jump. One ambiguous crossup can swing a round, so it's sometimes worth skipping guaranteed damage to set one up.
- **27 Knock them down first.** A knocked-down opponent can't anti-air as you jump. A good knockdown setup is a main goal of footsies.
- **28 Make them commit to a slow move, then jump over it.** For example: fake a throw after ticks and cross up their counter-throw; jump their 2MK when they're fishing for a super; or poke until their counter-pokes get rhythmic, then jump one.
- **29 Cause chaos, then cross up.** Do something unexpected and cross up while their reactions are off. Back off when they look desperate, or use options safe against their best comeback (a super they can afford).
- **30 Air-to-air and long-range jumps.** Stop their crossup with an upward normal and cross them up as they land. The winner of an air-to-air usually lands first and can rejump. Or jump over an opponent who jumps from far away.
- **Principle:** crossups belong to footsies only once the ground game is established. The ground game trains them not to expect jumps.
- **SF6:** knockdown oki (Cammy's Hooligan or Spiral Arrow knockdowns into a j.LK or j.MK crossup or safe jump) is Element 27. Element 28 is a jump over a whiffed 2MK~Drive Rush attempt or a slow heavy.

## Chapter 10: misdirection
Good fundamentals can be countered once they're predictable, so hide your patterns.
- **31 Meaningless whiffs.** A few times a round, whiff a harmless light. Opponents who whiff-punish your best poke misjudge the spacing and walk into it.
- **32 Break your timing.** Walk into 2MK range and don't press. Let them get restless, then hit them.
- **33 Sudden aggression.** Switch to all-out offense when they expect you to turtle, once you've read the flow.
- **34 Occasional wake-up 2LP.** It looks weak, so cautious players don't plan for it. Use it rarely. **SF6:** wake-up 4f jabs beat delayed or offbeat oki; per the player's wake-up rule this is often intentional.
- **35 Odd behaviour when losing.** Strange actions (stomping at mid range) can break their rhythm for a moment.
- **Principle:** observant opponents will find your patterns. Try something new each round; it only needs to be unexpected, not risky.

## Supplement A: projectiles
- A fireball is a slow, long-range poke (10+ frames of startup versus about 4-5f for good pokes) that makes up for it with a hitbox that can't be hit. Whiffing a normal leaves your limb exposed; a projectile doesn't, which changes the counter table.
- Standing back after a fireball gives up your own punish, because the projectile is still travelling. The further it travels before contact, the more advantage the thrower gets.
- Fireballs can be used like pokes: to pressure, beat mistimed normals, repel aggression and punish mistakes. Knockdown fireballs work as counter-pokes. Even minus on block, a blocked fireball pushes the opponent too far to punish.
- **Answers without a fireball:**
  - jump it (risky, but big reward);
  - stuff the startup with a fast poke from close enough;
  - build meter while closing in, so a super threat discourages fireballs;
  - trade with a long poke that reaches its full range in one frame, spaced so it trades after the fireball is out, as long as the trade favours you.
- **Test in training mode**: frame data alone misses hitbox details.
- **SF6:**
  - Drive Impact goes through fireballs.
  - Drive Parry and Perfect Parry on fireballs gain Drive (a perfect parry gives an advantage).
  - OD moves and some supers are projectile-invincible. Cammy's SA3 is anti-projectile (1-13f fully invincible); see the G1 R3 Critical Art through Sand Blast in the quick-dashboard review.
  - For Cammy, who has no fireball, the answers are: walk and block (bulldog), stuff it up close with fast pokes, parry it for Drive, jump only against far, slow fireballs, Drive Rush once they've committed, and save SA3 or CA for the fireball.

## Supplement B: avoiding footsies
Footsies are still the preferred foundation and need practice (shortcuts just create new weaknesses), and a strong opponent will drag you into mid range anyway. If you're losing the mid-range game to a better player, avoid it or neutralise it instead:
- **Extreme defense:** block a lot, walk back and get out at the first sign of danger. Stay well outside the dangerous mid range and frustrate them until they drop their plan. It dumbs the match down so ground fundamentals matter less.
- **Extreme offense:** dash in, cross up and keep up constant pressure, crossing the mid-range line and staying close. It rattles the opponent into matching your recklessness. It's inconsistent and gambling-based, but can beat strong players on a good day. Maj's caveat: good offense still starts from footsies, which let you move in and out of close range at will; without them you're only flipping 50/50 coins.
- **Screen position:** keep away from the corner against characters with strong corner pressure, and stay midscreen against characters whose damage depends on the wall.
- **Run away and build meter** when meter changes the matchup, and don't engage a player who has meter if one combo would kill you.
- **Takeaways:** blocking is safer than heroics; in tournaments, win by any sound means; in training, play footsies against stronger players, because hard losses teach more than easy wins.
- **SF6:**
  - Extreme defense means holding block and walking back while saving Drive for Drive Parry, Perfect Parry and Drive Reversal, and staying out of Drive Rush range.
  - Extreme offense means Drive Rush pressure and oki, at the cost of Drive and burnout risk.
  - Meter is Drive and super: winding the clock while building SA3/CA for a comeback is the SF6 version of "run away and build meter".
  - Corner avoidance matters most against characters with strong corner loops and wall-splat DI.
  - For Cammy, both routes exist: dive-kick and Drive Rush offense, or a patient, block-first approach. Name which one the player used when footsies weren't working.

## Epilogue: playing without fear
- When a veteran is reading your rhythm (sweeping your pokes, stuffing your long moves, pressuring you when you stand still), **stop and block for a moment** until they lose track of your habits.
- **Block with purpose, not fear:** blocking waits them out, drains their momentum, shows you their methods and sets up your next move.
- **Two ways to beat a strong footsies player:** out-think them at their own game, or build strategies that keep you out of their range (Supplement B).
- **The lazy intermediate trap:** a player who reacts only to where you're standing, ignores long-term patterns and doesn't believe footsies win matches stays stuck, winning on luck.
- **Don't let one bad exchange take your tools away:** if a stray counter hit beats your best poke, keep using it. Losing a little life is fine; dropping a key button limits you for the rest of the round.
- **Getting swept means you were outguessed once,** not that you'll be outguessed again. It's a small amount of damage, and you get back up.
- **Rule #5** (above): go all out, focus on the effort, and don't gamble without a reason.
- **SF6:** one counter hit or Drive Impact doesn't mean abandoning Cammy's best pokes (5MK, 2MK~Drive Rush); adjust the spacing or timing instead (Chapter 10). Blocking to reset a read is cheaper in SF6 than in older games because Perfect Parry and Drive Reversal are available from block, but watch Drive.

## How to apply this in a replay review
1. **Neutral (Elements 01, 04, 05, 20, 22, 31, 32):** log where each player walks in and out of the other's poke range and what that draws. Flag missed whiff punishes on step-in-and-out baits, and walks into range that weren't "earned" (Element 03).
2. **Reversal and super baiting (02, 06, 10-12):** at life leads or when the opponent has SA3/CA or Drive for DI/OD, note whether the player baited it (step in, block) or played into it. Count wasted supers and DIs on each side.
3. **Corner (07-09, Rule #2):** who controlled where the fight happened; how long each player stayed cornered; whether the player held the corner with checks and throws or gave escape routes; how he escaped it himself.
4. **Jumps and dive kicks (13-15, 23-26):** for each jump or dive kick, ask "what was it meant to land on?" Judge it as a read (did the opponent commit to something it beats?) or an unread gamble. Use the capture data's anti-air and dive-kick punish counts.
5. **Close range (16-18, 27-30):** walk-in strike/throw timing after plus situations, delayed strikes against defensive throws, crossup setups after knockdowns.
6. **Predictability (Rule #1, Chapter 10):** identify each player's repeated timing or spacing. Recommend one misdirection element when the player's pattern was punished, and use the opponent's pattern as a habit to cash out on.
7. **Gimmick check (Rule #4):** label the player's offense as tactic (a real 50/50) or gimmick, and say what's missing to make it a tactic.
8. **Mindset (Rule #3):** note visible state changes (after a big hit, low Drive, burnout, a life lead) and how each player's risk-taking changed.
9. **Avoiding footsies (Supplement B):** when a player was losing mid range, did they switch to extreme defense or extreme offense, and did it work? Did they manage screen position and meter for the matchup?
10. **Commitment (Epilogue, Rule #5):** flag half-hearted footsies (wandering at mid range just to survive), good tools dropped after one bad exchange, and blocking used to reset a read versus blocking out of fear.
